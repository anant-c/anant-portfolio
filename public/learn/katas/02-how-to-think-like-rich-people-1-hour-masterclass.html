How to Think Like Rich People (1 Hour Masterclass)

Varun Mayya 1:11:28 Watch on YouTube ↗
To build real wealth, you must shift from a mindset of seeking security through your own time and labor to becoming a free-market participant who buys freedom, owns assets, and leverages trust and reputation.

TL;DR

  • The fundamental drive of the wealthy isn't greed for money, but a desire for status, validation, influence, and freedom.
  • Wealth changes risk profiles: rather than seeking safe incremental returns, the ultra-rich prefer risky bets with extreme outcomes because they can afford multiple failures.
  • Rich people don't buy things; they unlock constraints by buying speed, attention, and the removal of annoyances.
  • True wealth is achieved by becoming a "free market participant" who decouples their personal time from income generation by owning assets that appreciate while they sleep.

"Blinking" — teleporting into people's minds

02:39 Varun introduces a technique called "blinking" (a concept from the game Dota for a short-range teleport) as a metaphor for teleporting into another person's mind to understand their motivations. He argues that the general internet consensus—that rich people are solely driven by the desire for more money—is fundamentally wrong. Investors and the wealthy have entirely different risk profiles. For example, a venture capitalist doesn't want a safe 10% return on a 1 crore investment; they would rather fund moonshot ideas that might fail completely but have the potential for massive multiplier returns. To successfully pitch or sell to wealthy individuals, you first have to blink into their minds and understand what actually drives them.

What rich people actually want

03:48 Social media and the press often misinterpret the intentions of the wealthy, spinning their actions as pure greed. Using Cristiano Ronaldo as an example, Varun points out that someone with a billion dollars does not play for the next paycheck or brand deal. Instead, the actual drivers are respect, validation, trophies, and the feeling of accomplishment. Wealth generation becomes a side effect of pursuing validation and status. This pattern holds true for many billionaires, such as Elon Musk, who would gladly lose a hundred million dollars if it meant gaining immense public validation. If you want to engage with the wealthy, assuming they just want to be marginally richer is a flawed strategy.

Traits of every successful person

08:36 Varun observes four consistent traits among the highly successful (net worth above $100 million):

  • Curious and excited to learn: They act like kids with new technology, whereas unsuccessful people often settle into victimhood, become nostalgic for a past that didn't exist, and aggressively reject the new.
  • Unafraid of making mistakes: They view mistakes as the necessary path to success, shrugging off the social conditioning that failure brings shame. They know you cannot reach success purely by avoiding errors.
  • Willing to make multiple attempts: They try things repeatedly. A good protocol is trying everything three times: first to get over the fear, second to stop sucking at it, and third to decide if they actually like it.
  • Learning from failure: Even when they fail, they extract a lesson. Jeff Bezos's Fire Phone failed, Elon Musk's early rockets exploded, and Ronaldo sometimes plays poorly, but their DNA compels them to stay in the arena and learn.

The 4 wealth zones

14:34 Varun maps out four distinct liquid net worth zones in India, detailing their different behaviors, aesthetics, and goals.

1 Cr — security & status

15:23 People at this level are highly security-focused and protective of their jobs and assets. They plan major purchases carefully. Their goals are stability for their family, a debt-free home, and emergency funds. A defining brand aesthetic here is the Nike Air Jordan—a visible symbol of success meant to fit in with the crowd. They are highly responsive to marketing that uses the label "luxury" because they desire the feeling of opulence.

10 Cr — freedom & recognition

18:26 At this tier, people have achieved financial independence and are comfortable in their own skin. They often choose subtle traditional aesthetics like an Indian kurta paired with Comet shoes. They take moderate risks, use private banking, travel business class occasionally, and own a luxury home. Their identity is still closely tied to their job (often CXOs or C-suite executives), and they highly value their reputation within their professional circles.

100 Cr — influence & access

22:57 This group consists of big-picture thinkers who handle large deals and feel the normal rules don't apply to them—they are willing to skip the queue. They focus on expanding their business empires and generational wealth planning. They have global LRS positions, write angel investment checks, and employ a wealth manager. They value experiences over material things, paying up to 10x more for "vibes" and exclusivity. They employ staff, have fractional private jet access, and wear subtle luxury items like distressed Golden Goose shoes. They buy influence and prioritize their relationship roster. However, their reputation is entirely dependent on their venture's performance.

1,000 Cr — legacy & trust

30:15 Visionaries and industry movers who operate with a macro view. They plan for multiple generations and focus heavily on philanthropy and societal influence. They operate through family offices and rarely see a bill, as a massive 20-person team handles their micro-decisions (such as checking in at an airport). They prioritize narrative control, news-making, and maintaining trusted relationships over earning the next crore.

Free market participation

36:56 The journey from 1 Cr to 1,000 Cr is a transition into true free market participation. A person with 1 Cr is "field-driven," entirely focused on performing one specific job (like playing cricket) to earn money. By the time they reach 1,000 Cr, they act as free market participants who can enter any industry they want (like MS Dhoni starting a hotel chain) without having to run the day-to-day operations. The key to wealth is unlinking your personal time and energy from the income you create, often by buying and owning assets.

Rich people buy freedom, not things

39:53 Wealth is not primarily used to buy things, but to unlock constraints. A private jet buys mobility; an assistant buys attention back; a lawyer buys certainty. Rich people evaluate an expense based on its ROI and the annoyance it removes, not its absolute price. A 5 lakh expense is cheap if it creates 5 crores of access. Varun notes a "middle class trap" where people hyper-negotiate small costs—like fighting an auto driver over 100 rupees—wasting their time and energy. For the wealthy, money is an ethereal energy redirected to buy other people's time or to sharpen their own mental bandwidth.

How the rich take risks

48:31 When the ultra-rich invest, they aren't looking for a safe 20% return. A person with 100 Cr would rather risk 10 Cr for a chance at making 500 Cr. Because they are highly diversified, they can afford multiple massive failures. Their goal is not avoiding risk, but figuring out how to survive failure while retaining unlimited upside. Varun shares a story of a businessman with a 150 Cr net worth whose main furniture company barely made profit; almost all of his wealth came from a single 15-year-old property investment bought by the government for 100 Cr. The lesson: massive upside often comes from side bets, provided you can survive the downside.

How to actually reach the rich

54:55 Cold DMs demanding things ("Help me, sir") do not work because they offer zero value and rich people are shielded from them. To reach ultra-wealthy people, you must create a system that generates undeniable excitement. Often, the best route is indirect: building a strong public brand, running a strategic billboard campaign where they will see it (like startups targeting Sam Altman in India), or having their trusted assistant discover your product (like a Valorant coaching app) and refer you upward.

Trust & proximity to power

1:02:36 Trust is where all the capital in the world lies. Proximity to power is more valuable than raw skill. If an influential person's trusted driver recommends an app they are using, that holds significantly more weight than a polished deck from a startup. Being consistently reliable in India is a bigger advantage than appearing brilliant, as many brilliant people lack reliability and decisiveness. Sometimes, doing strategic free work or a paid pilot for a reputable, top-tier client is the best way to prove usefulness and build a lucrative, long-term relationship.

Do this

  • Stop hyper-negotiating small expenses if they cost you significant time, energy, or attention.
  • Try any new technology or activity three times: first to get over the fear, second to stop sucking at it, and third to decide if you actually like it.
  • To reach someone wealthy and busy, don't ask for favors; build something incredibly useful and create "proof of work" that makes them excited to associate with you.
  • Focus on owning assets that generate returns while you sleep, rather than just adding more tasks that lock up your daily schedule.

Glossary

Blinking
A mental technique of teleporting into another person's mind to truly understand their motivations and risk profile.
Free market participant
Someone who has enough capital to enter and own a piece of any industry without having to personally operate the business.
Proof of work
A visible portfolio of past successful projects that demonstrates competence and reliability to potential high-net-worth clients.

Open questions / where I would push back

Note: Varun suggests that rich people are purely driven by validation, legacy, and influence rather than greed. While true for some, doesn't this perspective romanticize wealth and ignore how the relentless pursuit of "influence" can often be indistinguishable from greed in practice?
Note: The advice to do free work for exposure and trust-building works if you can afford to survive without immediate pay. How should a skilled but financially constrained individual navigate building this "proximity to power" without being exploited?
