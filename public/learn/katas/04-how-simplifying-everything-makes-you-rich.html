How Simplifying Everything Makes You Rich

Varun Mayya 0:28:51 Watch on YouTube

Doing more, building more, or adding more systems usually leads to confusion; the simplest version of a company or product almost always works best.

TL;DR

  • Audience attention is heavily fragmented; presenting a minimal, focused set of offerings is the only way to be remembered.
  • Adding people to a project often slows it down due to non-linear communication overhead (the Two-Pizza Team rule).
  • Engineers and managers naturally seek to add features and abstractions, but true value comes from building the single most vital feature and putting energy into sales.
  • Giving a talented person two different tasks often breaks their output quality; keeping them accountable for exactly one task yields dramatically better results.
  • Never automate a process until you have run it manually yourself enough times to fully understand its edges.

Why People Chase Complexity

00:53

We often assume that doing better requires building more complex systems. However, nothing is further from the truth. When Varun asked a room full of people if complexity is generally better, no hands went up. Yet, in practice, people behave as if it is. We see this acutely with the wealthy: as they gain capital and options, their lives become extremely minimalist—like Mark Zuckerberg wearing the same shirt every day.

In business, complexity creeps in as an attempt to look sophisticated or professional. But if you have 10,000 moving parts, your job is to dumb it down so that others can absorb it. The people whose businesses scale best are those who ruthlessly remove complexity from their own lives to manage the intense complexity of their companies. The simpler your company runs, the better it performs.

Steve Jobs and the Power of Focus

02:43

When Steve Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, the company had over 70 products. He slashed that lineup to just four focused offerings. He understood a fundamental truth about human bandwidth: you might be projecting 100% of your experience and work out into the world, but your audience is only absorbing 0.1% of it.

Varun asks random people who recognize him at the airport what they know about him. Person A will reference a video from a year ago; Person B will reference something from last week. Neither knows the whole picture. The "Toilet Model" captures this perfectly: we imagine our audience taking meticulous notes on our work. In reality, they are scrolling on the toilet, one hand on their phone, while their mom is calling them. Because of this fragmented attention, presenting 70 different stories is a guaranteed way to be forgotten. Focusing on four clear things makes it infinitely easier for the world to understand you, and for your team to align internally.

Why Large Teams Become Inefficient

06:14

Complexity often enters a system because of hiring. Consider what happened inside Varun's companies as they scaled to nearly 600 employees. He noticed that hiring an additional person didn't reduce the timeline. Instead, the task just split among three people, and they spent their days waiting on each other's deliverables. Out of a 600-person organization, perhaps only 50 key individuals are truly critical—these are the people actively reducing complexity rather than adding to it.

Jeff Bezos championed the "Two-Pizza Team" rule: any team should be small enough to be fed with two large pizzas, meaning roughly six to ten people. Anything larger introduces severe communication gaps and inefficiency. Crucially, each of these small teams must own exactly one goal. Giving a team multiple goals fractures their attention and guarantees failure.

Why You Shouldn't Build Everything Yourself

09:31

Engineers often want to build solutions from scratch. When someone asks, "Why use Zoho for CRM? Why not build it from scratch?" they are misunderstanding progress. Baking an apple pie from absolute scratch means reinventing the universe. The tools we rely on daily—from pre-mixed paints to third-party software—are abstractions that purposefully reduce our complexity.

If an artist insists that digital art isn't "real" art and that you must paint on canvas, they ignore the fact that someone else had to manufacture the canvas and mix the indigo paint for them. Being a "wrapper" on top of someone else's technology is often criticized, but it is actually a strategy to offload complexity. Startups win because they find a simpler, low-complexity way to solve a problem that an incumbent is bogged down trying to maintain.

Why Simple Ideas Perform Better

11:50

Society rewards appearing sophisticated, but simple ideas resonate far better with users. Varun notes that highly produced motion-graphics videos on his channel are routinely outperformed by a video featuring a simple slide deck. When he launched "God in a Box"—a tool that merely plugged the ChatGPT API into WhatsApp—he was shocked by its success. It was the dumbest, simplest thing he had ever built, yet it quickly gained millions of users.

Similarly, when running Scenes (a community startup), they constantly chased "weak product-market fit" by adding requested features. But users would inevitably abandon the complex platform for the simplest alternative: a basic WhatsApp group. The divide between successful and unsuccessful entrepreneurs often becomes apparent in their 30s. The unsuccessful ones keep building more unused features into their apps to justify their engineering titles, while the successful ones identify the single feature that works and spend all their remaining energy on marketing and sales.

Note: Complexity often serves as a crutch to mask a lack of true product-market fit or distribution.

The Hidden Cost of Changing Systems

18:08

This complexity bias also affects infrastructure. Someone might propose implementing a complex new NAS-loop LAN cabling system under the office desks simply because "that's how professional companies do it." But when management asks for the first-principles reason, there isn't one. The team has adopted an expensive, unmaintainable system merely to signal status.

Varun shares a story of an employee pushing to move all video editing to iPads. While it sounded innovative, it would have shattered their hiring pipeline, required a completely new software suite, and added hardware dependencies like styluses that get easily lost. It would have introduced enough complexity to shut the company down in a few weeks.

One Person, One Responsibility

24:02

Varun admits making this mistake as a young entrepreneur with limited cash flow. If a talented employee finished their work in six hours, he would assign them a secondary project for the remaining two hours. Inevitably, the quality of their work across the board deteriorated. Splitting focus destroys output.

Conversely, assigning one person to exactly one task enforces absolute accountability. The cognitive bottleneck frees up, and they produce far better work. A company scales much cleaner when it relies on one person per task—even if it means doing fewer things overall—than spreading its best people too thin.

Do It Manually Before Automating

25:49

Never automate a role or process before you have proven there is a manual need for it. When people pitch video editing software, they often fail because they haven't personally edited enough videos to understand the true spread of the work. Paul Graham's famous advice—"do things that don't scale"—applies perfectly here.

You have to edit ten videos manually yourself to learn the nuances before you can possibly automate it or hire it out. The same applies to AI avatars: shoot content natively for six months before trying to automate your likeness. You must intimately understand the game before you try to abstract it away. The best managers ensure the organization stays low-complexity as it scales.

Do This

  • Audit your product lineup or feature set: find the few things that actually drive value, and aggressively deprecate the rest.
  • Cap any single team's size at six to ten people. If a project requires more, split the goal and the team.
  • Assign every talented person exactly one primary responsibility. Do not overlap their tasks just because they have free hours.
  • Before automating any workflow or writing software for it, execute the entire process manually until you understand all edge cases.

Glossary

PMF
Product Market Fit; the degree to which a product satisfies a strong market demand. Weak PMF is often masked by constantly building unneeded features.
Wrapper
A business or software built entirely as an interface on top of someone else's underlying technology (e.g., an app built over ChatGPT's API). It succeeds by aggressively reducing complexity for the end user.

Open Questions / Pushback

1. Are there limits to the "one person, one task" rule?
While this works for execution-heavy roles (like engineering or video editing), doesn't early-stage company building require generalists who can handle product, sales, and marketing simultaneously before cash flow permits specialized hiring?

2. Does building a "wrapper" create fatal dependency risk?
By avoiding the complexity of building the underlying technology, you become entirely dependent on a platform you do not control. If the underlying platform changes its pricing or features, your simplified business model could break overnight.
