How to Get Hired Faster Than Everyone Else

Varun Mayya · 50 min · Watch on YouTube ↗

Hiring is about building a complementary team of individuals with unique superpowers and the right attitude, not finding interchangeable cogs, and for candidates, a strong portfolio and proven track record will always beat an AI-generated CV.

TL;DR

  • Hire like the Avengers, not Toy Story soldiers: Look for individuals with unique, un-fungible superpowers that complement the team's weaknesses, rather than treating people as interchangeable cogs.
  • Attitude dwarfs technical skill: While hard skills can be taught quickly, especially today, an unwillingness to learn or a poor cultural fit will doom a hire no matter their resume.
  • The traditional CV is dead: In the age of AI, words on a resume are meaningless; what gets you hired is a tangible portfolio or a strong referral that proves your capabilities.
  • Beware the pre-proof trap: Believing you have high potential is a comforting safety blanket, but demanding senior-level compensation before you've proven that potential is a massive red flag to employers.
  • Create a C-free zone: Retain your top "A players" by paying above-market rates and offering uncapped incentives, and fire your "C players" fast enough that you feel relieved when they're gone.

How Different Companies Hire

01:34 He argues that hiring is not a one-size-fits-all solution, illustrating this with a story about the Aditya Birla Group (AG). A conglomerate of 30-40 companies requires a very specific approach. Their strategy pairs highly technical, analytical young talent (often from institutions like BITS) with older, seasoned businessmen who deeply understand industry specifics (like cement) and P&L statements. This combination works perfectly for a generations-long family business, but it would look entirely different at Google or OpenAI.

The Fungibility Fallacy

04:22 Many organizations approach hiring as if employees are interchangeable—like green toy soldiers in Toy Story. The assumption is that you can plug any person into a role, and if they underperform, simply swap them out. He argues this is the absolute worst way to build a company.

Instead, companies should hire like the Avengers. Every individual should possess one unique "superpower"—a skill or trait that is extremely hard to find in the market, giving them an outsized advantage. While this means hiring people who have pronounced weaknesses in other areas, a well-constructed team complements these gaps, resulting in an aggregate force with no weaknesses. The "fungibility fallacy" is the belief that talent is easily swappable; in reality, once a person is trained and driving business value, replacing them is incredibly difficult.

The Hiring Stack

06:33 Contrary to conventional wisdom, hard technical skills fall at the very bottom of the hiring criteria stack. Based on 14 years of hiring experience, he outlines the true hierarchy of what companies look for:

  1. Attitude: If someone doesn't genuinely want to do the job, they will quit within months, regardless of their proficiency.
  2. Cultural Fit: Candidates aligned with the company culture are willing to adapt and learn new skills because they care about the overarching problem.
  3. Learning Agility: The world changes rapidly (e.g., the sudden integration of AI tools). The ability to learn quickly is non-negotiable.
  4. Ability to Understand: A surprising number of people fail to grasp explicit instructions and execute their own, incorrect ideas instead.
  5. Ability to Implement: Theoretical knowledge is useless without the capacity to spend weeks or months actually building the output.
  6. Leadership Ability: The skill to align people and move company goals forward through storytelling.
  7. Diligence: The ethic and care put into ensuring details don't slip through the cracks.
  8. Customer First, Sell Second: Doing what is right for the customer ultimately benefits the individual and the business.
  9. Technical Skills: Placed near the bottom because they are easily acquired if the candidate possesses learning agility.
  10. Aesthetic/Taste: In an era of AI-generated content, the ability to discern good output from bad is a premium skill.

He notes that you can evaluate a candidate across almost all these dimensions simply by giving them an AI tool and watching how they prompt it. Their approach will instantly reveal their learning agility, understanding, taste, and attitude.

Live Exercise with Hiring Team

15:16 To illustrate the reality of the job market, he brings in recruiters from across his companies. Their consensus highlights a structural problem in hiring: traditional CVs have lost their meaning. With AI capable of generating perfect resumes, words on a page no longer reflect actual competence.

Shria, who reviews hundreds of CVs weekly for creative roles, notes that candidates with flawless resumes often cannot put a basic sentence together on a call. For these roles, a CV is largely ignored in favor of a visual portfolio. Rohan, hiring for game development, echoes this: recruiters spend only 15-20 seconds on an ArtStation portfolio or showreel to determine if a candidate is viable. The biggest red flag is a candidate overselling a weak portfolio while demanding senior-level pay.

For intangible roles like sales or management—where portfolios don't exist—he advises asking candidates to explain past outcomes in extreme detail. Those who have actually done the work know the nitty-gritty; those faking it rely on fluff. Additionally, referrals remain the strongest filter because an employee putting their reputation on the line will organically filter for quality.

The CHAMPR Framework

27:16 He structures the evaluation of talent into a mnemonic framework called CHAMPR, which looks beyond raw technical ability to assess how someone will actually perform day-to-day.

Entry vs Senior Level Hiring

30:05 The core distinction in hiring stages is that entry-level candidates are hired for potential, while senior candidates are hired for proven expertise.

A massive mistake job seekers make is falling into the "pre-proof trap": demanding senior-level salaries based purely on self-assessed high potential, without the portfolio to back it up. He notes that choosing not to try is a "safety blanket"—a fake comfort that protects the ego but prevents the candidate from enduring the challenges required to actually prove their worth. He also calls out the age fallacy: the belief that getting older or having a family justifies a higher salary. The free market only pays for the value driven to the company.

Hiring Mistakes

31:37 He lists the most costly mistakes organizations make when hiring:

  • Rushing the process: Hiring the first person "with a pulse" because a role needs to be filled immediately almost always backfires.
  • Not vetting: Believing a CV without calling past employers to verify claims.
  • Ignoring culture fit: Every hire made with a bad gut feeling about culture fit has ended poorly.
  • Hiring for a "maybe" role: Bringing on a full-time employee when there is only a week's worth of clear work. This ambiguity guarantees disengagement.
  • Unstructured interviews: Without structure, you cannot compare candidates accurately. Rigorous interviews also create a sense of earned accomplishment for the candidate, making them value the job more.

A, B & C Players & Retaining Talent

37:20 He uses the "Quit Test" to categorize talent. If an employee walked in today and resigned, the manager's immediate emotional reaction tells them everything they need to know.

The organizational goal isn't necessarily a team of exclusively A Players, but rather a strictly "C-free zone." To retain A Players, the strategy is to pay above-market base salaries and offer uncapped incentive compensation. When an employee generates multiple times their salary in revenue, paying them generously is the easiest business decision to make. He notes that none of the top 16 people in his company have left over the last 3.5 years due to this philosophy.

Red Flag Syndromes

42:00 Once talent is inside the company, several behavioral "syndromes" are immediate red flags that warrant firing:

  • Victim Syndrome: Believing the company is exploiting them, despite operating in a highly competitive free market.
  • Messiah Syndrome: The belief that they are there to save their peers from management. He tells a story of an editor who started cooking Maggi for coworkers at night, claiming the company was neglecting them, as leverage for a raise.
  • Ostrich Syndrome: Ignoring reality or explicit client requests to instead build whatever they saw in a trendy tutorial.
  • Peter Pan Syndrome: An inability to act like an adult, constantly pushing minor, childish problems onto leadership.
  • I Know Best: Extreme confidence that usually stems from being untested in the real world.
  • Burnout Syndrome: Working to the point of exhaustion. He argues true burnout only happens when you are doing work you hate; people doing creative work they love naturally get lost in the process.

Do This

  • Build a visual portfolio or verifiable proof of work; stop relying entirely on a text CV to secure digital roles.
  • Treat technical skills as a baseline, but emphasize your learning agility and attitude in interviews.
  • Evaluate candidates practically by sitting with them and watching how they prompt AI tools for a generic task.
  • Conduct structured, rigorous interviews so candidates feel a sense of earned accomplishment when they pass.
  • Categorize your team via the "Quit Test" and fire fast enough that you maintain a "C-free zone" in your organization.

Glossary

Fungibility
The ability to swap one worker for another without any loss in performance or friction; a concept the speaker argues is a fallacy in modern knowledge work.
Applicant Tracking System (ATS)
Software used by recruiters to structure the hiring pipeline, track candidates, and manage referrals systematically rather than informally.
Pre-proof
A stage in a candidate's career where they possess high potential but have not yet generated the track record or portfolio to command senior-level compensation.
