The Most Valuable Skill After AI

Varun Mayya · 1:14:46 · Watch on YouTube ↗
When AI automates product creation, crafting a compelling, consistent story—world building—becomes the only defensible business moat.

TL;DR

  • A brand is a world: A brand is simply a story that enough people believe. It requires the same structural depth as a fictional universe.
  • Products are commodities: As AI makes building digital products practically frictionless, creating a believable world around that commodity is the only lasting defense.
  • Friction creates value: A functional world requires clear hierarchy, access barriers, unique language, rituals, and shaming for non-believers to feel real.
  • Leave room for myth: Leaving parts of the world open to interpretation—rather than explaining every detail—allows audiences to engage through their own theories and fanfiction.
  • Consistency over detail: Consistency in character behavior and overarching rules is far more critical than exhaustive planning.

The Catalyst: Products Are Commodities

01:10 The speaker argues that a brand is not merely sticking a logo on a product and expecting a premium. A brand is a compelling, real story that enough people choose to believe. The catalyst for this perspective was witnessing an AI (Claude Opus) perfectly recreate the mechanics of the game Rocket League in a browser using just a few prompts.

02:46 He argues that if AI can perfectly clone software and products, then the product itself becomes a commodity. What survives when everything can be cloned with a single prompt? Why do consumers pay a premium for Coca-Cola (sugar water) or a Rolex (a basic mechanical watch)? The answer, he claims, is the "lore." Players don't stick with Rocket League just for the driving physics; they stay for the esports championships, the recognizable cars (like McLaren), and the surrounding culture. The ecosystem—the world built around the product—is what retains value.

The Four Worlds of Reference

07:51 To dissect the mechanics of world building, the speaker uses four distinct case studies spanning fiction, luxury, entertainment, and business:

  • Dune: A massive, complex sci-fi universe.
  • Rolex: A high-end luxury watch manufacturer.
  • WWE: Theatrical, athletic storytelling masquerading as a sport.
  • AEOS: The speaker's own bootstrapped media tech company.

The Premise & Characters

10:07 He asserts that every compelling world is founded on a contradiction—a "yes, but" scenario. Dune is the most inhospitable planet in the universe, but it controls the most important substance in civilization. A Rolex is a simple, practical timekeeping instrument, but it serves as a massive public symbol of achievement. WWE is an athletic combat sport, but is entirely theatrical storytelling.

11:15 Within these worlds, he notes you need recognizable characters or archetypes. You don't need a specific person; Rolex uses archetypal personas like the executive, the explorer, or the diver. WWE relies on iconic characters like Stone Cold, John Cena, and the Undertaker. In a business context, the founders and key team members serve as these characters, playing specific roles within the corporate ecosystem.

Hierarchy, Entry, and Language

14:07 A world must have a sense of direction; inhabitants need to know how to move up or down, he explains. Dune separates power between the Emperor, the Great Houses, and the Fremen. Rolex uses pricing and model families (Datejust vs. Daytona) to signal status, giving entry-level buyers a clear ladder to look up to. WWE utilizes championships and "jobbers" (smaller wrestlers whose sole purpose is to lose quickly, providing a frame of reference for how powerful the main character is).

17:47 He emphasizes that a world is utterly useless if there is no barrier to entry. To be accepted by the Fremen, Paul Atreides must endure the brutal trial of learning water discipline. Rolex enforces a controlled purchase pipeline through official retailers, creating artificial scarcity.

20:07 Once inside, the speaker points out that the community is cemented through unique language. Outsiders shouldn't understand the lingo. Dune uses words like "spice" and "Lisan al Gaib." Rolex enthusiasts meticulously discuss "perpetual cyclops superlative chronometers." WWE relies on terms like "heel", "face", "kayfabe", and "pop". AEOS uses a blend of startup, poker, and gaming terminology like "GTM", "jamming" (spending more on a winning bet), and "tanking" (absorbing pain or delaying a decision).

Rituals and Shaming

23:43 Rituals elevate mundane actions into meaningful events, he observes. In Dune, the erratic sandwalk is a survival ritual. For Rolex, the purchase is heavily curated: the distinct smell of the boutique, the velvet couch presentation, and the ceremonial unboxing. WWE builds its entire brand on elaborate entrance walks, tolling bells, and in-ring contract signings.

28:25 Crucially, he argues there must be a penalty for non-believers: shaming. If a brand has no cost for breaking its rules, the story won't stick. In Dune, wasting water is fiercely shamed. In luxury circles, wearing a fake Rolex brings instant social ruin. In the WWE, breaking character or refusing to "go out on your back" (losing your final match to elevate a younger talent) is deeply disgraceful. In AEOS, running an unprofitable channel or pulling low view counts carries an intense social stigma that drives relentless performance.

Note: Shaming is often viewed negatively, but the speaker frames it as a necessary structural boundary. Without social consequences, the "reality" of the constructed world collapses.

Mechanics, Chance, and Lore

32:21 Worlds require structural laws (mechanics), he notes. Dune operates on the spice cycle; shields cannot be used in the desert because they attract worms. WWE operates on stipulated matches with clear victory conditions.

33:57 However, he claims strict rules must be occasionally broken by chance to generate dopamine. The random, terrifying appearance of a sandworm, the unpredictability of a rare Rolex model becoming available, or a surprise entrant at the Royal Rumble keeps the audience addicted.

36:39 The world's history is its lore, which he categorizes into three specific layers. Fixed lore provides the absolute, unchangeable facts. Flexible lore leaves room for subjective interpretation. Legend introduces unverifiable myths that build mystique.

Forking, Technology, and Eras

40:02 When factions disagree on the fixed lore (the canon), he explains that the world forks. Religions fork into differing sects. Bitcoin forked into Ethereum Classic and standard Ethereum. WWE forked into Raw and Smackdown. DC Comics regularly resets its canon (from the Silver Age to the New 52) when its characters become too powerful.

44:53 Technology also shapes the world, acting as a constraint that forces specific types of storytelling, he observes. Dune explicitly bans "thinking machines," forcing human evolution into new paths. WWE's reliance on handheld microphones dictates the aggressive, pacing style of its theatrical promos.

47:16 As worlds age, they pass through distinct eras, creating powerful nostalgia, he notes. WWE fans reminisce about the Attitude Era versus the PG Era. Apple fans remember the colorful iMac era versus the modern aluminum aesthetic. Consistent aesthetics—Dune's brutalist scale and yellow hues, WWE's neon rock, Rolex's green and gold polish—anchor these eras. Finally, leaving the world carries an exit penalty. Sell your Rolex, and your status evaporates; leave the WWE, and you forfeit the rights to your character.

Exposition vs. Interpretation

54:16 A critical mistake amateur world builders make is over-explaining (exposition). He argues that master storytellers use interpretation. By providing only sparse, opaque clues—like the lore delivery in games like Elden Ring or Returnal—audiences are forced to fill in the gaps.

He asserts that leaving relationships and histories ambiguous encourages the community to debate, theorize, and write fanfiction. This deep, interactive engagement is what makes a world sticky and expansive.

Story Machine vs. String of Pearls

58:02 Many creators build a "string of pearls," releasing a highly scripted event where time stands still until the next release. A superior approach, he argues, is building a story machine. By defining the rules, resources, and competing factions, organic conflict emerges continuously. Vlogs and real-world companies naturally operate as story machines because unscripted reality constantly throws up new friction.

Consistency and Retcons

1:00:52 In world building, consistency completely trumps intricate detail, he insists. Real humans are messy and contradictory, but fictional worlds collapse if characters act out of alignment without a massive inciting incident. If a character is portrayed as diligent and calculating, having them randomly forget something breaks the audience's trust in the world.

1:04:55 Because the real world of building a business is inherently chaotic, he notes that successful entities frequently retcon (retroactively change) their histories. When an actor in the Marvel universe runs into real-world trouble, the studio leans on the "multiverse" to seamlessly replace them. Similarly, startups that pivoted wildly and failed for years will later polish their origin story into a clean, linear narrative of visionary execution. Hindsight bias is actively weaponized to maintain the world's consistency.

The Ultimate Moat

1:08:12 World building creates internal constraints that drive incredible behavior, he argues. AEOS succeeds because its ecosystem convinces employees that massive distribution is achievable, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy. We rely heavily on social proof; if everyone around you believes the island is survivable, you will survive.

1:13:14 He concludes that in an era where AI can manufacture code, generate copy, and synthesize video instantly, the product itself is no longer defensible. Establishing a rich, consistent, and believable world around a commodity is the only competitive moat left. Without it, you are simply a cheap knockoff waiting to be replaced by the next automated generation.

Do This

  • Define your brand's core contradiction (the "yes, but") before writing any marketing copy.
  • Build clear barriers to entry so participants feel they have earned their access to your community.
  • Invent specific language and lingo that bonds insiders and naturally excludes outsiders.
  • Leave parts of your story unexplained so the audience can create their own interpretations (fanfiction).
  • Prioritize the consistency of your characters over an exhaustive level of detail.

Glossary

Jobber
A person whose sole role is to lose quickly to show how powerful someone else is; a frame of reference.
Kayfabe
Maintaining the illusion of a character in the real world to preserve the story's believability.
Heel / Face
The bad guy (heel) and good guy (face) archetypes within a narrative universe.
Pop
The sudden, loud audience cheer when a recognizable element (like entrance music) hits.
Canon
The elements of a story that the controlling entity officially dictates as absolute truth.
Forking
When an audience disagrees on what is canon, splintering into separate realities or sects.
Retcon
Retroactive continuity; changing past facts to make the present narrative appear linear and intentional.

Open Questions

Editor's pushback and questions for the reader:

  • Does shaming as a cultural lever inevitably lead to toxic environments, or is it an unavoidable boundary for any meaningful community?
  • If AI can generate lore, consistent histories, and language faster than humans, will world building eventually become a commodity too?
  • How much "forking" can a brand tolerate before the original identity is completely diluted?
