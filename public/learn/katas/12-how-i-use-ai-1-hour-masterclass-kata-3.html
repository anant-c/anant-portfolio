How I Use AI (1 Hour Masterclass) | Kata 3

Varun Mayya 44:30 Watch on YouTube ↗
Vague instructions force employees and AI to guess your intent; scale your leadership by providing exact context, references, and incentives instead.

TL;DR

  • Writing is active thinking. When your writing is unclear, your thinking is unclear. AI can mimic your style, but it cannot mimic your thinking unless you provide rigorous context.
  • Beware the "literal genie". Both AI and human employees will fulfill a vague brief by filling in missing context based on their own experiences. If their context doesn't match yours, the output will fail.
  • Senior employees are "context repair technicians." Companies often overpay for experience simply because management's briefs are underbaked. The employee's actual value is translating vague instructions into correct action.
  • Use AI to map edge cases, but humans to verify. Tools like GPT or Gemini are great for generating a dense list of costs or scenarios, but you must still make phone calls to get accurate, real-world numbers.
  • Design double-layered incentives. Don't micromanage. Set up a primary reward (e.g., hitting a sales target) paired with a constraint (e.g., keeping food waste low) so the system runs on autopilot without cheating.

00:00 The Illusion of Clear Thinking

Varun begins by challenging the popular idea that AI can simply "copy" you. If you upload ten documents of your previous work and tell an AI to write like you, it will successfully mimic your style. However, it won't end sentences the way you do or extrapolate situations correctly because it isn't mimicking your thinking. Most people write poorly because their underlying thinking is unclear.

The ultimate test of clear thinking isn't how it reads on a page—it's putting that thinking into action and seeing if it generates tangible results. Writing is the active mechanism that forces this clarity.

03:36 The Literal Genie Problem

To illustrate the core problem with delegation, Varun introduces a thought experiment about a "KD genie" (a shady, extremely literal genie). If you ask this genie for "infinite content ideas," it will jam your brain with so many flashing ideas that you physically can't stand still. If you ask for "infinite money," it won't quietly deposit it; it will literally drown you under a pile of physical cash or transfer ownership of every top company in the world to you, instantly crashing the global stock market.

To get what you actually want, you have to be excruciatingly specific: you must ask for exactly $500,000, delivered via non-fraudulent means, into a specific bank account without raising suspicion.

Both AI models and human employees operate exactly like this literal genie. If you tell an AI to "generate a picture of an ice cream," it has to pick a random flavor and color. If you ask your best friend, they will pick your exact favorite flavor because you share a deep context. When you give a vague instruction at work, the assignee automatically fills in the missing gaps based on their own context, which is often completely different from yours.

09:23 The Context Repair Economy

A core team that has worked together for years develops deep, shared context. They know what the leader likes, how they think, and can make operational decisions on their behalf. But how do you scale a company when new employees lack this history?

The traditional approach is to hire highly experienced people. Varun argues that companies often overpay senior engineers not because they type code faster, but simply because management provides "underbaked" briefs. The senior talent acts as "context repair technicians"—using their years of experience to figure out what the company actually needs when the instructions are vague.

Varun cites a classic Andy Grove anecdote: a boss asks a rookie to cook pancakes but completely forgets to say "golden brown on both sides." The rookie cooks one side, serves undercooked goo, and the customer complains. The failure wasn't skill; it was a missing specification. By writing down precise examples, checklists, and specifications, you eliminate the need to overpay for context repair. You can align a new employee's mental model with yours from day one, allowing even inexperienced people (like the team that built AOS) to perform at a high level.

16:56 Forcing Context with Deep Research

Varun demonstrates how to apply this abstraction practically by simulating a new pancake business in Hebbal, Bangalore, with a budget of ₹50,000 per month. Instead of just generating a generic plan, he uses AI for "deep research."

When prompted with this vague brief, the AI immediately returns with vital context-gathering questions: Will there be a physical location? What kind of pancakes? Do you have existing equipment? What are the operating hours? Will you cook yourself or hire a chef?

Note: Good AI prompts act as a mirror, forcing you to think harder about the operational details you neglected to specify in your own head.

Once you provide those answers, the AI generates a number-dense projection. However, Varun insists on performing the "AI Test." The AI might estimate a head cook's salary at ₹15,000, but you must verify it. You walk into a local restaurant, ask the chef their salary, and maybe discover the real number is ₹30,000. AI maps out the edge cases exhaustively; humans must still verify the reality via phone calls.

23:13 Designing the Double Incentive

Instead of micromanaging (acting like a "Zumba trainer" endlessly cheering people on to do their jobs), a leader's true job is to set up systems that run on autopilot. If you struggle with this, you can ask AI to design an incentive structure that prevents cheating.

For the pancake business, the AI designed a two-layered, self-regulating system:

  1. Primary Goal: Hit a sales target of ₹70,000 to unlock a 5% bonus pool (split 60% for the head cook, 40% for the helper).
  2. Constraint Check: Maintain a food cost below 30%. If food waste exceeds this, the sales bonus is completely forfeited.

This ensures employees don't artificially inflate sales by carelessly wasting ingredients or cooking recklessly to hit raw volume.

26:05 Unreliable Narrators

When performance metrics rely purely on surface-level observation, you fall victim to the "unreliable narrator." Varun shares an internal example from his company (YAS):

He noticed two new editors were taking a full day to edit a single 30-minute shot. An outside observer might see them working late into the night (e.g., 9:00 PM) and conclude they are overworked. But investigating the inputs revealed the deeper root cause: they lacked experience and were spending 50–60% of their time just looking for B-roll footage. An experienced editor handles the exact same task with excellent work-life balance. Adjusting company policy around the inexperienced editor's struggle creates long-term inefficiency.

32:19 AI for Mood-boarding and References

Never expect your team to know what is in your head. If you want a scene where a character has a panic attack, don't just describe it vaguely. Ask AI to find movie references. It might pull up the driveway collapse from The Sopranos or the dinner scene from Iron Man 3, detailing the exact camera cuts.

You can then feed these references into tools like Nano Banana to generate comic book panel frame-by-frame breakdowns. Some might call this stealing, but executing a copied concept with your own camera, actors, and specific constraints inherently turns it into something original.

37:45 The Sunshine Test

Scale requires ruthless clarity. Don't let low performers wonder where they stand. Send them an explicit email detailing their current output versus the exact expected output, with a concrete timeline for improvement.

As a benchmark for high-output leadership, Varun outlines his own daily communication volume: roughly 20 emails, 80-90 work WhatsApp messages, and 1-2 Google Docs just to keep coordination moving.

Finally, run your operations through the "Sunshine Test": If an action or decision you made was printed on the front page of a newspaper tomorrow, would you be ashamed? If yes, don't do it. Assume everything you do is public.

Do This

  • Write out explicit spec documents and examples before delegating tasks to new employees or AI.
  • Validate AI-generated assumptions (like budgets or salaries) by talking to real humans in the industry.
  • Replace manual micromanagement with double-layered incentives (e.g., hit a sales metric, but forfeit the bonus if waste is high).
  • Identify "unreliable narrators" by inspecting the inputs (why are they working late?) instead of just judging the output.
  • Apply the Sunshine Test: ensure every instruction, firing, and decision could comfortably be published on the front page of a newspaper.

Glossary

Literal Genie
A metaphor for an AI or an employee who executes a vague instruction exactly as stated, filling in the missing context with unpredictable or harmful assumptions.
Context Repair Technician
An expensive, highly experienced employee whose primary value is deciphering and correcting management's poorly written briefs.
Deep Research
The practice of using AI to generate comprehensive edge cases, follow-up questions, and projections that force a human planner to specify missing context.
Unreliable Narrator
An employee whose performance metrics appear skewed (e.g., working late) because management measures output time instead of diagnosing input bottlenecks (like lacking templates).
The Sunshine Test
A decision-making filter where you ask if you would be ashamed if your actions or feedback were published on the front page of a newspaper.

Open Questions

Is an over-reliance on explicit instructions going to stifle an employee's ability to develop their own taste? If you spell out every constraint, you might prevent them from exploring better alternative solutions.

If senior engineers are mostly "context repair technicians," does writing perfect briefs make senior talent obsolete? Or does it free them up to solve genuinely novel problems instead of interpreting management's confusion?
