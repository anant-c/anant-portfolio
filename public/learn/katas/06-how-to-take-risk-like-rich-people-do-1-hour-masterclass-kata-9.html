How To Take Risk Like Rich People Do

Varun Mayya 1:07:45 Watch on YouTube

Success doesn't come from blind gambles; it comes from running cheap experiments, staging your risks over time, and always carrying a shock absorber.

TL;DR

  • Every decision is a risk, but we mistakenly outsource our risk assessment to the crowd (doing what everyone else does feels safe, even if statistically dangerous).
  • Professional risk-takers stage their bets: they run cheap experiments with high payoffs and buy more certainty over time instead of going all-in on day one.
  • Never take a leap without a "shock absorber" (a large cash runway and deep transferable skills) to survive the worst-case scenarios.
  • If your work relies heavily on luck (apps, content), run many small parallel bets. If it relies on skill (surgery, video editing), rely on extreme, deliberate practice.
  • The ultimate antidote to risk is entering a non-competitive, unsexy market; the lack of competition gives you the time needed to build a moat before anyone catches up.

Everything in life is risk

03:33 Varun observes that most people mistakenly define a risk as trying a new, unproven idea. In reality, everything is a risk—even eating food carries a statistical risk. However, we navigate the world by crowdsourcing our risk tolerance. If everybody else is grinding for college entrance exams or breathing polluted air in Delhi, we subconsciously classify those activities as safe because the herd accepts them. We rely on the crowd rather than actually calculating the odds.

Furthermore, Varun argues that the popular image of the founder is flawed. Note: The media often romanticizes entrepreneurs as wild gamblers taking huge leaps of faith. An entrepreneur's actual job is not to seek out danger, but to relentlessly reduce it. The goal is to find an opportunity that looks obvious to you, test it as cheaply as possible, and scale it only when it works—making it appear risky to outsiders but calculated to you.

Timeless human nature vs. permanent shifts

09:29 Citing Morgan Housel’s book Same as Ever, Varun points out that human behavior has barely changed in 300,000 years. The brain's apparatus—the amygdala, the prefrontal cortex—remains functionally identical. Humans are universally greedy, fearful, overconfident, tribal, and short-sighted. For instance, the best-selling candy bar in 1962 was Snickers, and it remains a top seller today because taste and human desires endure.

However, the technology we use to satisfy those desires changes rapidly. The transistor radio of 1962 is gone, replaced by smartphones and AI. Crucially, when a massive behavioral shift does take hold (like the adoption of the internet or AI), it is permanent. A smart strategy is to ground your career in timeless human behavior (e.g., people craving autonomy and mastery at work) while constantly adapting to the latest technological tools.

Think in probabilities, not binaries

14:35 Risk is simply what is left over after you think you've calculated everything. The average person evaluates opportunities in a binary way: it will either succeed or fail. Probabilistic thinkers, on the other hand, assign percentages (e.g., a 20% chance of success and an 80% chance of failure).

While it's impossible to predict exact odds—nobody could have predicted a scandal that abruptly devalued degrees from Galgotias University—attempting to model the percentages is still highly valuable. Varun recommends using AI tools like ChatGPT to research market variables and run probability models for different career moves based on historical data.

GTO and Staged Risk

16:18 Borrowing from professional poker, Varun advocates for Game Theory Optimal (GTO) play: finding very cheap, ideally free experiments that carry a massive potential payoff. The value of a cheap experiment is that you can shut it down without ego or sunk costs dragging you down into a deeper hole.

This is precisely how startup venture funding is structured to mitigate disaster:

  • At the Seed stage, a founder has an idea but hasn't proven anything. Investors might put in 2 crores for a massive 50% equity stake because the risk of total failure is at its highest.
  • By Series A, the team and product are proven. The company's valuation jumps, and new investors might put in the same 2 crores but only receive 5% equity because the existential risk has dropped.
  • In Late stages, the company is valued purely on its revenue multiple.

Instead of taking suicidal leaps in your own life, stage your risks just like a venture capitalist stages funding. Run the cheap experiment first to clear the map, and only invest heavily once you see what you are doing.

Two-year career outcome modeling

27:40 When comparing two opportunities, humans are notoriously short-sighted. Suppose you must choose between a monitor sales job paying 60,000 rupees a month and a finance role paying 30,000 rupees a month. Varun advises looking two years out and modeling four scenarios:

  • Best-case: You become VP of sales at the monitor company, versus leading client relations in the rapidly growing finance sector.
  • Average-case: You stagnate at your current salary in both.
  • Worst-case: The monitor company goes bankrupt due to commoditization, leaving you with useless skills. The finance company gets caught in a scandal; you lose your job but retain valuable industry experience.
  • Tail-risk: Unpredictable events like a sudden health issue.

This modeling reveals why freelancing for tiny startups is so dangerous. Small startups hire freelancers precisely because they cannot afford the risk of full-time employees. By working exclusively with them, you inherit their massive failure rate. If they fold, your two-year outcome is decimated.

Always have shock absorbers and avoid medium risk

41:38 You must take risks to win, but you shouldn't jump without a shock absorber. This means building a cash runway (e.g., 10 lakhs in India) and broad, transferable skills. Wealthy people take seemingly huge risks (like Justin Kan starting a failed legal tech company after selling Twitch) because their personal shock absorbers make the worst-case scenario irrelevant to their lifestyle.

Varun warns fiercely against medium risk. A medium risk is starting a project that you calculate will take 3 months and cost 3 lakhs, when you only have 6 lakhs in the bank. If it actually takes 8 months to find your first customer, you run out of runway halfway through and are entirely wiped out. You must practice "productive paranoia"—always assume things will go wrong and massively over-capitalize your runway. The ultimate shock absorber in any business is having a vast pipeline of leads.

Three stories on randomness and wealth

45:12 Varun shares three anecdotes that highlight how much of wealth generation relies on random gambles rather than careful planning:

  • The Furniture Salesman: A man on a plane flying business class ran a low-margin furniture franchise. But his net worth was in the hundreds of crores because he and his friends bought a random piece of cheap real estate at age 23, which the government later acquired for a massive sum.
  • The Infosys Shares: A man bought shares of Infosys early on simply because Narayana Murthy pressured him as a friend. Today, that random favor funds his next five generations.
  • Venture-Backed Founders: In the startup world, heavily funded founders often take steady salaries and invest in their friends' startups. They know their own company might fail due to tail risks, so they offset the risk. Many make more money from their side investments than their primary business.

Loss aversion and the 90/10 rule

50:56 Humans suffer from loss aversion: losing hurts twice as much as winning feels good. Varun explains that he no longer gives out superficial promotions or promises projected earnings to his team, because failing to hit those numbers feels like a devastating loss to the employee, eclipsing any actual gains.

To combat this, Varun uses a barbell strategy: keep 80-90% of your portfolio or business ultra-safe (like predictable B2B services). Use the remaining 10% to take wild, high-payoff risks (like new experimental ventures). When a high-risk bet doubles or triples in value, act like a private equity investor: sell a portion of your stake immediately to recoup your initial capital, and let only the pure profit ride.

Skill domains vs. random domains

1:00:10 You must adjust your risk strategy based on the rules of your environment.

In high-randomness domains (like building consumer apps, making viral content, or venture capital), the outcome is largely disconnected from pure effort. The winning strategy is to run many small, parallel experiments and shut down the failures quickly. In high-skill domains (like surgery or video editing), outcomes rely entirely on mastery. The winning strategy here is extreme, deliberate practice across multiple scenarios.

The ultimate antidote to risk

1:02:01 In poker, "multi-way variance" dictates that at a table of nine players, it is highly probable someone has a better hand than you. If you play head-to-head, your odds drastically improve.

In business, Varun notes this translates to entering highly unsexy, non-competitive markets. Because risk is crowdsourced, everybody flocks to building AI wrappers or SaaS apps because it feels socially acceptable. If you decide to build a B2B network-attached storage solution or an office tiffin service, you might be the only one in the room. This lack of competition acts as a seal, granting you the crucial two years needed to refine a mediocre product while enjoying a monopoly.

Do this

  • Model your next two years: Before accepting an offer, chart the best, average, worst, and tail risks on a timeline instead of just looking at the starting salary.
  • Set a hard shock-absorber number: Calculate exactly how much cash you need to survive for a year in your worst-case scenario, and never dip into it for a "medium risk" project.
  • Pull initial capital early: The next time a risky investment or side project doubles in value, take out your initial stake and let only the profit ride.
  • Match your strategy to your domain: If your project relies on algorithms and virality, run five tiny variations. If it relies on technical craft, spend that same time practicing one variation deeply.

Glossary

Tail Risk
Highly improbable, outsized events (like a sudden health crisis or global pandemic) that most predictive models ignore.
Deep Optionality
The ability to withstand any risk because you've mastered universally transferable skills, rendering you adaptable to any situation.
Game Theory Optimal (GTO)
A poker concept applied to life: making mathematically sound decisions—specifically, cheap experiments with massive potential payoffs—regardless of what the competition is doing.
Loss Aversion
The cognitive bias where losing a resource feels significantly more painful than gaining that exact same resource feels good.
Stated vs. Revealed Preference
The gap between what people claim they want (stated) and what their actual actions and spending prove they want (revealed).
