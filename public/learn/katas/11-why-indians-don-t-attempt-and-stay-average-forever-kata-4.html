Why Indians Don't Attempt

Varun Mayya 54 min Watch on YouTube ↗

True competence is built through high-risk attempts and failures, not quiet niceness, and predicting it is crucial for hiring and growth.

TL;DR

  • Competence can be accurately measured (like a "scouter" level) by looking at a person's history of taking high-risk swings, facing skepticism, and either failing cleanly or pulling it off.
  • Indians systematically misread competence because the culture overvalues quiet, conflict-avoidant, "nice" introverts, whereas real impact requires salesmanship, charm, and fighting inertia.
  • Excuses stop mattering at higher levels of responsibility; the gap between believing you can do something and proving it is wider than most people think.
  • Luck is a critical component of all massive success, but luck only finds you after you attempt seriously and repeatedly. Action produces information.
  • Your growth ceiling in any job is capped by your manager's scouter level; highly competent people only want to cluster with other highly competent people.

05:12 The Two Startup Indias

There are effectively two distinct games being played in the startup ecosystem. The first game ("Startup India 2") is populated by everyday founders building modestly profitable businesses, often judging the other game from the outside. The second game ("Startup India 1") features the major players—companies like Zomato, LensKart, and Cred—who raise enormous amounts of capital and are frequently unprofitable for years.

When you sit outside the second game, it is easy to view these high-burn companies with skepticism, assuming they are reckless or simply lucky. But they are playing a fundamentally different, all-or-nothing game: going for a monopoly position by swinging for a six on every ball. You cannot gain access to this upper tier by merely networking or thinking you deserve it. Acknowledgment only comes after you grind and produce miracles that prove your worth. The gap between thinking you possess the potential to succeed and actually proving it in the real world is measured in years of effort.

Note: The speaker touches on an essential truth about prestige. Prestige and access are trailing indicators of competence, not leading ones. You are only granted the permission to play the high-stakes game after winning repeatedly in the low-stakes one.

14:10 How to Build Competence

Competence is not an abstract trait; it is forged through action. Mayya introduces a framework for how individuals level up their "scouter read" (a Dragon Ball reference to a device measuring power levels). The process is a specific, repeatable loop of taking on personal risk.

The crucial element in this cycle is personal risk. If you are a billionaire's child taking a risk with parental money, your successful outcome yields a lower competence read from your peers because the cards weren't stacked against you. Conversely, real risk means facing severe professional or financial consequences for failure.

Mayya tells the story of an executive at a major gaming publisher who had the authority to write a massive check to fund a game, but hesitated. The executive explained that while he was empowered to make the bet, if the game failed in the market—even due to unpredictable macro conditions—he would automatically lose his job. That dynamic creates true personal risk, forcing the individual to ensure their competence is aligned with their conviction.

Interestingly, you can still level up your competence even if you fail, provided you take a clean attempt. A clean attempt means putting in genuine effort and, when failure is inevitable, raising your hand to honestly diagnose why it didn't work. Rather than silently quitting your job or blaming external factors like a broken AC or a tight budget, you update your mental model. People respect a clean failure and will often give you another shot. If you do not swing at all, you get "predictive competence" at best, which is ultimately worthless without execution.

20:55 The Five Scouter Levels

The speaker categorizes professionals into five distinct tiers based on their history of attempts and risk-taking. These levels are an internal compass used by highly competent people to instantly read the room and decide who to work with.

At the bottom are the NPCs (Level 0), who merely consume content without holding strong beliefs or taking any action. Just above them are the Commentators (Level 1). India has an abundance of commentators—people burning their energy on Reddit and Twitter, holding very strong opinions about how things should be done, but possessing zero personal P&L (Profit and Loss) or history of actual execution.

The turning point is Level 2: The Local Doers. These are the freelancers and side-hustlers who take small initiatives. They operate with low personal risk but manage to get some wins. Even if commentators mock them online, highly competent people respect Local Doers because they have crossed the threshold from talking to doing.

Level 3: Founders and Owners represent those taking repeated, high-risk swings with real skin in the game. They endure multiple failures before finding success. You cannot claim an owner mindset if you demand a full market salary while simultaneously demanding equity; true ownership requires putting your own downside on the line. Finally, Level 4: Kingmakers are individuals who have accumulated so much social capital that a single failure barely dents their reputation (e.g., Elon Musk). They enter projects holding all the cards and can move markets with a single decision.

Crucially, money does not perfectly correlate with these levels. An NGO founder who successfully organized a mass social movement sits at Level 3, earning far more respect from the competent class than a Level 1 commentator who got rich off a lucky cryptocurrency investment.

40:02 The Indian Competence Blindspot

India as a nation struggles to read competence accurately. The cultural ideal of a successful, competent professional is heavily skewed: we imagine a quiet, shy, introverted, highly technical, and conflict-avoidant engineer working tirelessly behind the scenes while a louder co-founder unjustly takes the credit. This is a reflection of national insecurity and the overproduction of engineers.

The reality of business building is the exact opposite. Companies are massive engines of conflict and inertia. The most competent people—the ones actually generating positive P&Ls—are highly verbal, charming, willing to engage in conflict resolution, and operate primarily as salespeople. They push against the natural human desire to nap and do nothing. Being "nice" is just table stakes; it is not a substitute for the ability to move things forward.

If you optimize for your own comfort, you will naturally dislike highly competent people because they will kill your bad ideas, ask uncomfortable questions about KPIs, and disagree with you openly. Indians also tend to attribute the success of others to luck ("their parents were rich") while attributing their own minor successes to raw skill. In reality, luck is a byproduct of taking multiple hard shots.

Note: The speaker touches on the "Schadenfreude community"—people bonding over the failure of others. Engaging in this actively destroys your ability to accurately read competence, reducing evaluating talent to a bitter, envious social process rather than an objective analysis of risk-taking.

30:38 Action Produces Information

There is a paralyzing belief that you must possess perfect information before making a move. Many professionals hold back from trying because they are afraid to find out the truth—what Mayya likens to being too anxious to visit a doctor for a cancer screening. But as Brian Armstrong noted, action produces information. You don't need to know the entire map; you just need to do something, and the answers will reveal themselves.

By taking an attempt, you generate the data needed to adjust. When Mayya's company began producing content, they did not have a clear monetization strategy. The strategy emerged organically as a result of consistently publishing and observing what resonated.

Furthermore, fear of ridicule keeps people locked out of success. Mayya tells a story of meeting Mark Zuckerberg during the live demo of the Ray-Bans smart glasses, where several things visibly failed on stage. Zuckerberg was not embarrassed; he simply noted, "I'm happy we got three out of five things right." He was completely comfortable with public failure. Similarly, the CEO of Qualcomm faced immense ridicule when they first introduced CDMA technology. In India, however, the fear of this exact ridicule keeps 99% of the population from ever attempting hard things.

49:15 How to Raise Your Scouter Level

If you want to escape the trap of staying average, the formula to raise your personal competence reading is straightforward:

  • Pick a measurable game: Ensure your outcomes are objectively measurable (revenue, users, watch time). Avoid roles where success is entirely subjective or heavily dependent on fifty other people.
  • Ship in public: Let people see you struggle. When you go completely silent for months, it signals that you are failing and hiding it. When you struggle openly, competent people will often step in to help you.
  • One clear win is permanent: A single undeniable success permanently shifts how the market views your competence, earning you the benefit of the doubt for years. You only need one success to change your baseline.
  • Beware the Scouter Penalty: If you make the exact same mistake multiple times without updating your mental model—like an engineer building an app a week hoping for a viral hit in the wrong market—your scouter level drops sharply. The most competent people own their failures brutally. They say, "I chose the wrong market, my fault" or "The pandemic happened, my fault for not having a contingency." They never blame external variables.
  • Cluster with competence: Your growth ceiling is hard-capped by your manager's scouter level. A low-competence boss will suppress your growth. Highly competent people only want to cluster with other high-competence individuals, often skipping small talk entirely to connect on a deeper level of execution.

Do This

  • Evaluate your current manager's competence. Your career growth is maxed out at your boss's scouter level; if they cannot take big swings, you cannot either. Ask for a team switch if necessary.
  • When hiring, stop overvaluing the brand names on a CV. Dig exclusively into what they personally contributed and the specific, high-risk attempts they took.
  • Audit your past failures. Did you blame the market, the funding environment, or your co-founder? Rewrite the narrative to take 100% accountability for the failure.
  • Identify the "nice" trap in your behavior. Are you avoiding necessary workplace conflict just to preserve a reputation as the "good guy"?

Open Questions

Is high personal risk always necessary?
The framework insists that without personal downside, a success isn't truly respected. But isn't there merit in asymmetric bets where the upside is huge but the personal risk is algorithmically minimized?
Does the "nice" trap apply globally?
The speaker attributes the conflict-avoidant, "nice" ideal to Indian culture, but corporate bureaucracy worldwide seems to reward survival-via-niceness over aggressive risk-taking. Is this uniquely Indian or uniquely corporate?
