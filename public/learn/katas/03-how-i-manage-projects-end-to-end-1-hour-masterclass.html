How I Manage Projects End To End

Varun Mayya 0:43:20 Watch on YouTube ↗

You don't know what you're building when you start. Clarity comes from taking action, building a low-resolution team first, and scaling your organizational chart iteratively.

TL;DR

  • Start low-resolution: Accept that you don't fully understand the market or the product; action is what produces information.
  • Build a minimum viable team: Break the project into problem-oriented departments rather than predetermined roles.
  • Categorize into Sure Shot vs. R&D: Scale known methods with capital, and focus intense founder attention on fast trials for unknowns.
  • Scale the org chart iteratively: Find the exact shape of your team through playable vertical slices (Alphas); lock in permanent hires only when the shape is proven.

The Low-Resolution Start

02:04 Whether you are building software or a physical product, every project begins with a blurry, low-resolution photograph of the market and the desired outcome. The trap is assuming you can plan your way to clarity. He argues that action produces information. The more you build and interact with the real world, the more resolution you gain.

Why Waterfall & Agile Are Outdated

03:17 Traditional project management frameworks fail in an environment of rapid technological change.

  • Waterfall operates on the assumption that the map is already known—that you possess a perfect specification of what the customer wants.
  • Agile assumes your immediate backlog is known, allowing you to plan sprints a few weeks out.

In 2026, both assumptions are deeply flawed. Because distribution and AI change constantly, you need a different model: one where the plan and the team gain resolution simultaneously.

Break Projects Into Departments, Not Roles

06:31 When structuring a new initiative, start by defining departments. A department represents a problem that needs solving (e.g., cinematics, sound). A role is a learned solution (e.g., Director of Cinematics).

If you naively copy the structure of large incumbents, you will inherit their bloated middle management—layers of staff whose only purpose is disaster prevention. Instead, form a Minimum Viable Team. For his game Unleash the Avatar, this meant assigning exactly one person to each department. The goal here is breadth, not depth.

Sure Shot vs. R&D

09:43 With your departments mapped out, label every single one as either "Sure Shot" or "R&D".

  • Sure Shot: The method is understood. Scaling it is simply a matter of spending money to hire more people. Every dollar spent yields predictable output.
  • R&D: The method is unknown. This demands heavy founder attention and rapid trials. The goal is to compress months of research into intensive 5-day experiments. Because R&D is a gamble, keep roles low-cost and rent technology rather than buying it.

Crucially, you cannot innovate everywhere. Choose only one or two segments of your project for R&D. If your entire operation is experimental, a three-month timeline will stretch into four years.

Vertical Slicing & Density

14:18 Instead of building sequentially, develop a "vertical slice"—a small, fully functional segment of the project. In software and games, these iterations are Alphas.

Between Alpha 1 and Alpha 3, you use your low-resolution team to build a slice, test it, and learn. Through this process, you discover the project's "density." You identify gaps between existing roles and fill them with complementary hires. The actual shape of your organizational graph emerges organically from the work, not a whiteboard session.

Note: The case study on photogrammetry (18:03) illustrates this perfectly. They started scanning local slums with mobile phones, moved to DSLRs, realized the assets didn't fit the game, and finally flew a dedicated team with drones to a film town. The early, cheap iterations dictated the expensive, high-resolution solution.

When to Invest in Technology

21:46 Technology is a lever for scale, but it must be justified by capacity math. For instance, hand-keying animations for their game would require 40 animators (costing roughly 4 crores a year). Investing 50 lakhs in motion-capture gear meant they only needed seven animators. The break-even was obvious.

The core rule: Rent technology during R&D; buy technology for Sure Shots. You buy tools when you want to lock in a process and permanently reduce headcount costs.

The Role of AI in Projects

25:11 Generative AI should accelerate the internal workflow, not serve as the customer-facing product. A game where the core art and dialogue are fully AI-generated feels like junk to players. Instead, retain humans for the creative, audience-facing elements, and use AI to build custom CRM tools, align shared state across disjointed departments, and manage communication.

Distribution & Funding

31:03 Publishing your work early acts as a magnet. Putting out trailers early in development yields brutal but necessary feedback, attracts top-tier talent (who wouldn't join an invisible project), and convinces global mentors to guide your in-house staff.

Funding should strictly map to your team's resolution:

  • Seed: Capital to find the shape of the organization. You have the freedom to fail and redraw.
  • Series A: Capital to scale the specific parts of the shape that you have proven.
  • Series B: Unlimited fuel for a fully locked, high-resolution organizational chart.

He warns heavily against scaling prematurely. If you lock your final shape, raise massive capital, and then realize you were wrong, you are trapped. Redrawing the shape requires returning to a low-resolution team, which means firing people you just hired.

40:44 Ultimately, a successful company's "playbook" isn't a Google Doc of procedures. The true playbook is the organization chart itself—the specific network of permanent staff, contractors, and embedded muscle memory that executes the work.

Do This

  • Label every department as Sure Shot or R&D.
  • Limit R&D to only one or two segments of your project to avoid infinite timelines.
  • Use temporary contractors to find the shape of a role before making permanent hires.
  • Invest in technology (buy) only when you know the capacity math to reduce long-term costs.
  • Use AI for workflow and internal alignment, keeping the core product human-made.

Glossary

Minimum Viable Team
A foundational team structure emphasizing breadth across required departments rather than depth, used to find the initial shape of a project.
Vertical Slicing
Building a small, fully functional segment of a project (e.g., a 5-minute playable game demo) to test and learn before scaling.
TAM (Total Addressable Market)
The theoretical maximum number of customers or users your project can strategically reach.
Playbook
Not merely a document of procedures, but the proven organizational chart and network of individuals that executes the work.

Where I Would Push Back

Open Questions:

2. The rule of only investing in technology once the math is proven makes sense for Sure Shots. But what if a transformative piece of technology is required during the R&D phase just to see if a novel method is even possible?
