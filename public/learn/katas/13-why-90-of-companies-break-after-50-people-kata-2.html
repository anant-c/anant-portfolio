Why 90% of Companies Break After 50 People

Varun Mayya · 1:00:14 · Watch on YouTube

The hardest problem in scaling after early growth is coordination; leaders must structurally lower coordination costs by generating current through clear writing, storytelling, and designing direct paths to mastery.

TL;DR

  • Scaling inherently degrades quality because it is impossible to make every new employee care as much as the founder; the only scalable solution is developing core leaders who propagate the culture.
  • A highly coordinated team of average performers will consistently destroy an uncoordinated team of superstars who are fighting for status.
  • A leader's primary operational job is to lower the "cost of coordination" for each team member by actively removing blockages (such as low skill, poor communication, or high ego).
  • Beyond basic survival, cash is an extraordinarily weak incentive; true and lasting motivation comes from respect, status, purpose, and ultimately, a structured path to mastery.
  • Clear writing is the ultimate organizational cheat code: it explicitly lowers coordination costs by providing unambiguous direction, effectively substituting constant synchronous meetings.

The Challenge of Scaling Quality

The chronic worry of any founder at scale is losing control of output quality. At 500 employees, leadership no longer knows what the newest hire is actually doing on a daily basis. Even middle managers often lack visibility into what their two-level skip-down reports are actively producing.

This challenge is particularly lethal in services businesses. The natural instinct of young founders is to aggressively scale: you secure one project, immediately chase a second, and by the time you land the third, the quality of the first completely collapses. To survive, a business must standardize its output so that the 1,000th employee provides the same care and quality as the founders did in their apartment.

02:59 The problem is that you cannot physically sit with every single one of your 500 employees and make them care. The prevailing cultural narrative tells people not to care about their jobs. Because no one else cares, a company that manages to sustain actual care for its clients naturally outperforms the market. The only proxy to achieve this at scale is creating really good core leaders who absorb the founder's DNA and intentionally pass it along to the next layer of management.

The Coordination Cost Framework

To understand why companies break, you must understand the mathematics of teamwork. Consider the multiplayer game Dota. While technically a five-versus-five game, it often feels like nine-versus-one because all ten players secretly believe they are the best player being dragged down by their teammates. If you take a team of absolute superstars who lack coordination and pit them against a highly coordinated team operating at a fraction of the skill level, the coordinated team will rip the superstars apart.

11:58 This dynamic explains why most startups explode. It is not a lack of funding or market fit; it is five co-founders who eventually decide they want to pull the company in slightly different directions.

Every team operates under a Threshold of Effective Coordination—the maximum friction allowed to still produce a high-quality outcome. Every individual brings their own Cost of Coordination. Person A might be deeply arrogant and refuse to collaborate. Person B might be highly collaborative but unable to speak the dominant language of the team. Person C might be a great cultural fit but severely lacking in technical skill.

A leader's core responsibility is to drop the cost of coordination. They must act as a massive unblocker. If a team member cannot communicate effectively, the leader must intervene and build a bridge. In extreme cases, like Elon Musk walking the factory floor, a leader approaches a blocked employee, asks what is stopping them, and physically removes the obstacle—whether that requires a stern conversation with a peer or directly calling Jensen Huang to secure a shipment of 100 GPUs.

Generating Current as a Leader

To reduce entropy and lower coordination costs, a leader must act as a generator of current. In a Zumba class, a room full of people might be exhausted at 7:00 AM, but the instructor provides an artificial burst of relentless energy. The participants absorb that current and perform the routine.

There are four primary ways to generate current in an organization:

  • Excitement: rallying the team around the pure thrill of a new capability or project.
  • Incentives: structurally rewarding specific outcomes.
  • Inspiration: attaching the work to a massive, world-changing goal.
  • Fear: the metaphorical "teacher's duster," holding people strictly accountable for failures.

Consistently generating current is exhausting. There will be days when the leader is entirely depleted. But a true leader must show up and generate current anyway, because the 16 core people relying on them need that energy to pass down to the rest of the organization. Over time, the best leaders train their teams to absorb and independently generate their own current.

The Power of Stories and Incentives

22:05 "Setting incentives is a superpower. Five words that change how I think about almost everything: runs on superpowered incentives."

The operational units of current generation are stories and incentives. Consider physical items: a pair of company sneakers or access to an employee lounge are fundamentally useless. However, by attaching a narrative to them—stating that only those who have served three years and hit specific milestones earn access—you imbue them with immense value. Stories give weight to the mundane.

For storytelling to work, a leader must have a streak of success. If a leader promises a future outcome and consistently delivers it over three years, their storytelling becomes prophetic. The team will follow them into uncertainty. Conversely, a streak of failures renders storytelling completely unreliable. Leaders must still take hard shots with a 50% failure rate to push boundaries, but they rely on their established credibility to survive the misses.

When designing incentives, leaders frequently fail by assuming their employees want what the leader wants. Incentives must perfectly align with an individual's internal story fit. For example, some employees place zero value on a cash bonus but will dramatically increase their output if incentivized with a four-day workweek. Another team member might not care about prestige in the local tech hub; their primary driver might be becoming the first person in their rural village to earn a substantial salary. A leader must investigate and align with these highly specific, deeply personal aspirations.

The Quest for Mastery

The startup ecosystem heavily relies on throwing cash at top talent, yet venture-funded companies in India regularly watch their highest-paid executives walk out the door. Cash is the weakest long-term incentive.

Money is critical only up to the point of survival—the threshold where an employee feels they are earning fairly relative to their peer group and are no longer stressed about bills. Once that baseline is crossed, the drivers of human motivation shift dramatically upward.

After cash, employees seek Respect (general consensus that they are capable). This evolves into a desire for Status (an absolute ranking within their field). Next comes Purpose (finding inherent meaning in the daily work). Finally, at the apex, is the quest for Mastery.

Mastery is the strongest hook in existence. Video editors inherently want to become motion graphics designers, not because it pays more (most corporate clients do not need extreme motion graphics), but because it represents a higher tier of skill. Dota players spend thousands of hours grinding for Matchmaking Rating (MMR) without being paid a dime.

Note: The speaker repeatedly references Dota (Defense of the Ancients), a highly competitive multiplayer strategy game where precise team coordination is strictly required to win.

When you provide a clear roadmap to mastery, you put an employee in the "lightning state": a zone where they are perfectly challenged, slightly out of their depth, but fully aware of the exact steps required to reach the next level.

Be extremely cautious with financial incentives. As detailed in the book Punished by Rewards, heavily tying external rewards (cash) to tasks an employee intrinsically enjoys will eventually destroy their internal motivation. They will stop coding or designing for the love of the craft and will only execute when paid to do so.

Clearing Human Blockages

If a leader's job is to drop the cost of coordination, they must ruthlessly hunt for blockages. In any growing company, entropy introduces friction. Recognizing the type of human blockage dictates how you solve it:

  • Low Skill: The easiest blockage to fix. In an environment with high talent density, individuals can be rapidly upskilled (e.g., a junior scripter successfully pivoting into a highly capable video editor).
  • Poor Attitude: Extremely difficult to fix. This is often deeply rooted in an individual's background or upbringing. They inherently view work as a scam and actively drain the team's energy.
  • Attention to the Wrong Details: Employees who fixate on minor imperfections (a tiny visual glitch) while ignoring massive structural failures (the entire video's pacing is ruined).
  • Low Accountability: The chronic blame game. Fostering high accountability requires a leader to ensure that admitting a mistake won't immediately result in termination.
  • Highly Transactional Behavior: Individuals who refuse to operate an inch outside their strict compensation parameters, crippling the team during weekend emergencies.
  • Victimhood: A perpetual belief that the universe, the market, and the company are actively conspiring against them.

Clear Writing as a Coordination Cheat Code

44:52 "I think the best leaders are writing all the time clear precise communication because I've seen teams but nobody knows what to do next."

There is a definitive cheat code to artificially lower the threshold of coordination: clear writing. Most teams stumble not because of malicious intent, but because the path forward is ambiguous. When a team of five people sits in a room unsure of the next step, entropy wins.

By writing extensively, a leader maps the exact route. If you send a detailed document with specific voiceover pointers, clean audio requirements, and clear structural guidelines, you completely eliminate the need for a synchronous meeting. You bypass the risk of miscommunication entirely. The best leaders, regardless of team size, heavily document their systems and expectations. If you write well, you can achieve the same outcome with one-tenth of the meetings.

The Four Horsemen of Closed Systems

Companies are fundamentally "delusion bubbles." They require a suspension of reality to function; if everyone objectively assessed the failure rate of startups, no one would ever clock in. To keep this bubble intact, a closed system relies on four pillars, much like an organized religion:

  1. Direction and Shared Stories: The equivalent of a holy text. It explicitly outlines the values, the goals, and the myths the group shares. When two people inside the system meet, they instantly recognize their shared alignment.
  2. Celebrations: The rituals and festivals. Marking milestones together physicalizes the progress of the company.
  3. Rules and Enforcement: Establishing rules is meaningless without consequences. Giordano Bruno was burned at the stake for challenging his closed system. While draconian, a company must enforce its boundaries; if a leader refuses to fire someone chronically missing targets, the entire storytelling structure collapses because the team sees that the rules are fake.
  4. External Validation: The bubble requires outside oxygen. Clients spending real money or the media acknowledging the company's success provides the critical external validation that proves the internal delusion is actually working.

42:45 "The highly coordinated team in Dota or any other game will rip apart the team of superstars if they're fighting."

While you should aggressively protect the bubble, recognize the 100% Coordination Myth. It is impossible to achieve total alignment across hundreds of people. The goal is to isolate and remove the extreme "red aura" individuals who actively generate friction, while accepting an 80-90% baseline of coordination that still leaves room for internal debate and experimentation.

Do This

  • Write clear, highly precise instructions for major tasks to eliminate ambiguity and artificially drop your team's coordination costs.
  • Map out the progression path for your team members so they can clearly visualize the steps required to achieve mastery in their role.
  • Align your incentives with the personal, internal stories of your team members, rather than projecting your own motivations onto them.
  • Actively identify and remove human blockages (low skill, bad communication) for your team rather than waiting for them to figure it out alone.
  • Enforce the rules and swiftly let go of chronic non-coordinators to protect the integrity of the team's culture.
Threshold of Effective Coordination
The maximum acceptable level of friction a team can endure and still produce high-quality, standardized output.
Current Generator
A leader who intentionally provides the energy, direction, and motivation needed to overcome team entropy and drive action.
Delusion Bubble
A closed system or company culture held together by shared stories, celebrations, and heavily enforced rules.
Red Aura
A term for an individual who actively resists coordination, breeds low trust, and creates chronic friction within the team.
